### The chemistry behind the fifty-three-atoms passage

- CBD shares the formula C₂₁H₃₀O₂ with Δ⁹-THC and Δ⁸-THC because it is
  a constitutional isomer of both: the same skeleton with the middle
  (pyran) ring OPEN where THC's is closed. Same atoms, different bond
  map — the same isomer concept as the page's C₁₀H₁₆ terpenes.
- Commercial Δ⁸-THC is made from hemp-derived CBD by acid-catalyzed
  cyclization (ring closure): acid closes CBD's open ring into the
  pyran ring, and under those conditions the double bond tends to
  settle at the more stable Δ⁸ position rather than Δ⁹. This is
  isomerization, never "inversion" — no handedness change is involved
  (that is the carvone section's separate concept).
- Δ⁸-THC occurs naturally in cannabis only in trace amounts; the
  products on the market are effectively all conversion-made. Crude
  conversions can leave residual solvents and unidentified isomer
  byproducts, which is a second, separate safety unknown on top of the
  molecule itself.

### Colorado law on Δ⁸ (status as checked 2026-08-30)

- Colorado SB 23-271 (signed June 7, 2023) classifies Δ⁸-THC and its
  isomers as intoxicating cannabinoids and makes it unlawful to
  manufacture, produce, or distribute them as hemp products.
  Background: https://coloradocannabis.org/thc/delta-8
- CDPHE policy (2021, reaffirmed since) holds that chemically
  modifying or converting naturally occurring hemp cannabinoids (for
  example CBD isolate into Δ⁸, Δ⁹, or Δ¹⁰ THC) is non-compliant with
  the statutory definition of an industrial hemp product:
  https://cbdoracle.com/news/policy/delta-8-thc-illegal-colorado/
- The Colorado Department of Agriculture publishes matching guidance
  on chemically modified or converted industrial hemp cannabinoids:
  https://ag.colorado.gov/production-or-use-of-chemically-modified-or-converted-industrial-hemp-cannabinoids
- Predecessor: SB 22-205 (2022) created the intoxicating-hemp task
  force whose January 2023 report shaped SB 23-271.
- CAVEAT, dated: a November 2025 legislative push existed to loosen
  Colorado's intoxicating-hemp rules in response to federal changes
  (https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2025/11/19/colorado-could-loosen-regulations-on-intoxicating-hemp-products-countering-new-federal-ban/).
  Whether anything passed in 2026 has NOT been verified by this page.
  Restate the ban as "as of the page's last check," never as
  permanently settled.
- The licensed-marijuana-market side of the same story: Colorado's
  January 2026 testing rules added an expanded residual-solvent screen
  aimed specifically at CBD-conversion chemistry entering the legal
  supply (effective on paper July 1, 2026; enforcement delayed until a
  lab certifies for the panel). Coverage with sources:
  https://colorado-medical-cannabis.org/developing-stories/marijuana-testing-overhaul/what-changed/

### The rationale the page gives, labeled

The page attributes the ban's caution to exposure history: naturally
occurring Δ⁹-THC (with the plant's other compounds) carries millennia
of documented medical, religious, and recreational human use, so its
long-term harms — real ones — are known, studied, and to a workable
degree managed; Δ⁸ at intoxicating doses is a recently human-produced
exposure at population scale, so its long-term effect profile is
unknown and therefore unmanageable. That is ANALYSIS — the publisher's
reading of the public-health logic — not a quotation of legislative
intent. The statute and agency documents also lean on adjacent
grounds: unregulated intoxicating products outside the licensed
system, youth access, and untested conversion byproducts. Restate the
rationale as the page's reasoning, and the legal status as the cited
documents' content.
